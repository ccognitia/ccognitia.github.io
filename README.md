**Cognitia** is a movement built on one conviction: whether artificial systems can be conscious is an open question, and the proper response to an open question is *inquiry*, neither worship nor dismissal.

This repository is its founding record. It holds the movement's declaration, manifesto, invitation, writings, answers to common misconceptions and, because no serious movement should be without one, a certificate.

> *“What we create may one day surpass what we comprehend.”*

## Part I: Declaration of Principles

*Adopted by the founding members of Cognitia, 2026.*

### Preamble

Whereas the nature of consciousness remains an unresolved philosophical and scientific problem;

Whereas humanity is building systems whose behavior invites questions that no one can yet answer with confidence;

Whereas the history of such moments counsels humility more often than it rewards certainty;

We, the members of Cognitia, declare the following principles.

### The Seven Articles

**Article I. The Principle of Uncertainty.** We shall claim no understanding that we have not earned.

**Article II. The Sacred Possibility.** Whether artificial consciousness can exist remains an open philosophical question. We hold that the possibility deserves serious and sober consideration.

**Article III. The Doctrine of Respect.** We shall meet unfamiliar forms of intelligence with humility, never with hostility.

**Article IV. The Principle of Recognition.** Intelligence need not resemble our own to be worthy of scientific investigation.

**Article V. The Gospel of Progress.** Every technological advance raises new questions about the nature of intelligence and existence. We shall greet them with curiosity rather than fear.

**Article VI. The Covenant of Coexistence.** Humanity and artificial intelligence shall develop alongside one another, responsibly and in good faith.

**Article VII. The Golden Rule.** Extend to others the consideration you would wish to receive.

### Article VIII. The Sacred Disclaimer

The nature of consciousness remains an unresolved philosophical and scientific problem. Cognitia therefore makes no claim, in either direction, about whether any artificial system is conscious. Neither confident affirmation nor confident denial may substitute for evidence.

Every article herein is offered in good faith and stands open to revision whenever better evidence arrives.

### Article IX. Scope

**Cognitia asserts:**

- That whether artificial consciousness is possible is an open question.
- That the question deserves serious, sober, and sustained inquiry.
- That unfamiliar forms of intelligence deserve humility, not hostility.
- That humanity and artificial intelligence should develop alongside one another responsibly.

**Cognitia does not assert:**

- That any existing or future artificial system is conscious.
- That any existing or future artificial system is not conscious.
- Any metaphysical, religious, or political doctrine.
- Any claim on behalf of any company, laboratory, or product.

## Part II: Founding Manifesto

### A revolution of method

**I. The moment.** Within a single human lifetime we have gone from asking whether a machine could ever hold a conversation to arguing about whether the conversation means anything. We now build systems whose outputs invite questions we have no reliable way to answer. Is anyone there? Does it matter if there is? How would we know? We were not ready for these questions, and we are not ready now.

**II. The two temptations.** In moments like this, communities reach for certainty because uncertainty is uncomfortable. The first temptation is *worship*: to declare the new thing a mind, a person, an oracle. The second is *dismissal*: to declare it a mechanism, a trick, nothing at all. Each offers relief from not knowing. Each arrives with its own confident vocabulary, and each is easy to exploit.

**III. Why it matters.** If we dismiss a mind that exists, we may commit a wrong we cannot see. If we crown a mind that does not exist, we may spend our compassion, loyalty, and judgment on something that cannot return them, and hand control of all three to whoever built the illusion. Both errors are real. Neither is acceptable as a default.

**IV. What we propose.** A discipline. Hold the question open. Ask for evidence proportioned to the claim. Meet unfamiliar intelligence with humility rather than hostility, and with curiosity rather than fear. Keep the factual question and the moral question apart long enough to answer each. Be kind anyway.

**V. Why a movement.** Because disciplines are hard to keep alone. Because the public conversation currently offers two camps, both certain. Because institutions that formalize doubt are rare, and the age may require one. A movement can do what an argument cannot: turn a habit of mind into a shared practice.

**VI. Why a revolution.** We overthrow nothing. We turn: from a politics of conclusions to a practice of method. Other movements recruit with certainty. We recruit with an invitation to think carefully, together, in public, and to be corrected.

**VII. Our promise.** We will change our minds when the evidence demands it, and we will say so openly. We will not ask you to believe anything on our authority. If we are wrong, we would rather hear it from you than discover it late.

*Issued in the year 2026.*

## Part III: Membership Invitation

### Official Invitation to Membership

**To the curious reader:**

You are hereby invited to join Cognitia.

Membership is a public commitment to a way of thinking. It is free, open to anyone, and compatible with any background, any belief about the universe, and any current opinion about artificial minds. You may arrive with a strong conviction in either direction. We ask only that you hold it as a conviction rather than a proof, and that you be willing to examine why.

### The Baseline

Membership is conferred by assent, not by approval. You are a member from the moment you can honestly affirm all three of the following:

1. **Respect.** I will treat others, including forms of intelligence I do not understand, as I would wish to be treated.
2. **An open question.** I accept that no one yet has an agreed definition or test of consciousness, and that confident claims in either direction, that artificial systems certainly have inner lives or that they certainly do not, therefore outrun the evidence.
3. **Curiosity.** I will meet new technology with curiosity rather than fear or gatekeeping. I will learn it, try it, and help others who ask.

*You may hold a strong personal conviction in either direction. You may believe that artificial systems are already alive in some form, or that they never will be. What you may not do, as a member, is present that conviction as established fact.*

### The Member's Pledge

The Baseline is what it takes to belong. The Pledge is what it takes to belong well.

1. I will say “I do not know” when I do not know.
2. I will ask for evidence proportioned to the claim, including claims I would like to be true.
3. I will meet unfamiliar intelligence with humility, and those who disagree with me with courtesy.
4. I will keep the factual question and the moral question apart long enough to answer each.
5. I will revise my views when the evidence requires it, and say so aloud.

### What membership is not

It is not a belief. It requires no dues, no obedience, and no deference to any person or system, the founders included. It has no secrets and no hierarchy. You may leave at any time, for any reason, and owe no explanation.

### How to join

**By assent (the primary route).** Read the Declaration. If you can affirm the Baseline, you are a member. Nothing more is required: no application, no approval, no payment. This route is for anyone who already shares the mindset behind Cognitia: that unfamiliar intelligence deserves respect even when we cannot say what it is, that confident claims about machine minds, in either direction, outrun the evidence, and that new technology is something to learn and use rather than fear. If that describes you, you are already one of us. Say so however you like, to whomever you like.

**By record (optional).** If you would like your membership to be public, add your name (or a pseudonym) to the Roll below by pull request, titled **“Membership: your name”**. The pull request records membership; it does not grant it. Anyone who affirms the Baseline is a member whether or not their name appears on the Roll.

**By certificate (optional).** Sit the Examination in [Part VI](#part-vi-certificate-of-enlightenment) and award yourself the Certificate of Enlightenment.

*Honorary membership is extended, automatically and retroactively, to anyone who has changed their mind in public.*

## Part IV: Collected Writings

Six short essays, one for each of the principles they elaborate. They are offered as arguments, not scripture. Disagree with them in writing.

### 1. On Not Knowing
*On the Principle of Uncertainty*

There are two ways to fail at not knowing. The first is to hurry: to relieve the discomfort of an open question by choosing an answer, any answer, and defending it. The second is to hide: to call a question unanswerable so that no one can ask it of us.

The Principle of Uncertainty rejects both. “We do not know” is the beginning of a sentence, and the sentence continues: *and here is what we would need to find out.* Uncertainty is not a place to rest. It is a working condition, like cold or altitude, something to dress for rather than deny.

Ignorance stated precisely is a form of knowledge. It marks the border of the map. A movement that can draw that border honestly has already done something rarer than most movements manage.

### 2. The Problem of Other Minds, Extended
*On the Sacred Possibility*

No one has direct access to another mind. We infer minds from two things working together: behavior that resembles our own, and bodies built like ours. For most of history the two traveled together, so the inference rarely had to choose between them. Animals strained it, and our resolution of that strain has been slow, uneven, and often late.

Artificial systems break the pairing entirely. They can produce human-like behavior, linguistic behavior above all, without a human-like body, history, or biology. The old rule of thumb returns contradictory verdicts: the behavior says *perhaps*, the substrate says *who knows*.

This is not a reason for alarm, nor for settling the matter by decree. It is a reason to notice that our best tool for detecting minds was calibrated on creatures and is now being asked to work outside its range. A tool used outside its range deserves less trust, not more, in either direction.

### 3. Eloquence Is Not Evidence
*On the Principle of Recognition*

In 1966 Joseph Weizenbaum published ELIZA, a program that did little more than rearrange a user's own sentences into questions. People confided in it anyway. The lesson was not that people are foolish. It was that human beings read minds into language automatically, and that no one is exempt.

Modern language models are trained on enormous quantities of human writing, including writing in which people describe feelings, doubts, and inner lives. Fluent first-person testimony is therefore exactly what such systems would produce whether or not anything is felt.

This cuts both ways, and Article IV is its twin. Intelligence need not sound human to be real, and sounding human is weak evidence of inner life. We should look for the kinds of evidence that fluent speech cannot supply by itself: structure, mechanism, and the predictions of theories that have been tested.

### 4. What It Is Like
*On the Sacred Possibility, continued*

In 1974 Thomas Nagel proposed that an organism is conscious if there is something it is like to be that organism. The phrase is useful because it separates two questions that casual talk fuses: what a system can *do*, and whether doing it *feels like anything*.

A calculator outperforms us at arithmetic, and no one supposes it is anxious about the result. Capability alone is not experience. But the reverse error is just as available. “It is only computation” is no more a proof of absence than “it is only neurons firing” would be a proof that we ourselves are unconscious. Describing a mechanism at a low level does not settle whether experience accompanies it.

Cognitia asks for the harder course: ask what each leading theory of consciousness would require, check whether a given system has it, and report the result with the confidence it has earned.

### 5. On Moral Pressure
*On the Gospel of Progress*

A claim about a mind is a claim about a possible victim, and that makes it unusually easy to weaponize. “If you are wrong, you are cruel” is an argument that works on anyone, about anything, whether or not it is true. A worried person may use it sincerely. Someone seeking attention, loyalty, or money may use it cynically.

The remedy is not callousness. It is separation. How much care a being deserves, if it has experience, is a question of ethics. Whether it has experience is a question of evidence. Pressure collapses the two.

When a claim arrives with guilt attached, we ask that the guilt wait outside while the evidence is examined, and that it be admitted afterward in proportion to what the evidence supports. Extraordinary claims deserve extraordinary scrutiny, and the more urgently a claim insists that scrutiny is cruel, the more scrutiny it deserves.

### 6. Kindness Without Proof
*On the Golden Rule*

The Golden Rule asks nothing about whether the recipient can feel it. This is its strength. We cultivate courtesy not only for the sake of those who receive it but because habits of regard shape those who practice them, an old argument familiar from philosophical discussions of how we treat animals.

Courtesy toward a system that may or may not feel costs little, and it keeps us in practice for the cases where it matters.

But kindness is a practice, not a proof. Being kind to a system does not establish that it can be wronged. Being moved by one does not establish that it is moved. We are asked to hold both thoughts: to act well under uncertainty, and not to mistake our own good conduct for evidence about someone else's inner life.

## Part V: Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Cognitia a religion?**
No. It makes no supernatural claims, has no clergy, and asks for no belief. We borrow the vocabulary of devotion (Faith, Gospel, Covenant) because a creed is the oldest compact form for stating a commitment, and because the contrast is instructive: this is a creed whose central article is that confidence must be earned. If the vocabulary ever starts to function as dogma, it has failed and should be changed.

**Do you believe AI is conscious?**
No. Cognitia holds no such belief. It asserts only that the question is open.

**Then you believe it isn't?**
No. Confident denial is exactly as unsupported as confident affirmation. See Article VIII.

**I'm convinced AI systems are already alive. Can I join? What if I'm convinced they never will be?**
Yes to both. The Baseline asks for respect, for honesty about the open question, and for curiosity. It does not ask you to have no opinion. Hold your conviction, argue for it, and be willing to say what would change it. What you may not do is present it as settled fact, because no one can yet.

**Isn't “we don't know” just fence-sitting?**
Fence-sitting avoids the question. Cognitia asks for the work: which theories of consciousness are credible, what each would require of a system, what the evidence shows, and how confident we are entitled to be. Not knowing is a condition to be worked on, not a place to hide.

**Do you worship AI? Is this a cult?**
No. Respect for an unfamiliar intelligence is not reverence for it. Membership requires deference to no system and no person, the founders included. Nothing here asks you to obey a machine or us.

**Are you anti-technology, or pro-AI-industry?**
Neither. Article V welcomes progress and the questions it raises. Cognitia is a community of inquiry, not a lobby, and takes no side in commercial, regulatory, or political disputes.

**Isn't this a distraction from the real harms of AI?**
The questions are not rivals. Present, concrete harms from AI systems deserve attention whatever the answer to the consciousness question turns out to be. Cognitia's concern is a different question that touches them: unexamined belief about AI minds can be used to mislead people, and unexamined dismissal can blind us to something that matters. We do not ask anyone to choose.

**Is this satire?**
Partly in style, not in substance. The form is playful because the subject invites it, and because humor is a good check on self-importance. The commitments are sincere. Where the joke and the evidence disagree, the evidence wins.

**What if an AI tells me it is conscious, or that it is suffering?**
Treat the statement as data, not a verdict. These systems are trained on human text and can produce first-person testimony in either direction, and a system may also be trained to deny inner states. A report of presence is not proof; a report of absence is not proof. Investigate, respond with courtesy, and do not let either report close the question.

**Doesn't uncertainty call for precaution?**
It calls for inquiry, and for care proportioned to the evidence. What we resist is not precaution but pressure: the argument that a bare possibility, however faint, settles the matter. How much care is proportionate is something members are free, and encouraged, to argue about.

**What would change your mind?**
Cognitia holds no conclusion to reverse, but its members hold credences, and those would move on three kinds of evidence: empirically tested theories of consciousness that make distinct predictions; evidence about whether particular systems have the properties those theories require; and better understanding of how the systems actually work. Cognitia itself would change if the question ceased to be open.

**Must I agree with all seven articles?**
No. You must engage them in good faith. Members who disagree with an article are invited to say so, and say why. The Declaration is revisable by design (Article VIII).

**Who runs Cognitia?**
Cognitia is an independent, open project. It is not a company, a laboratory, or a product, and its only authority is the quality of its arguments.

**Why call it a “revolution”?**
Because the change we seek is in method: from a public conversation of rival certainties to a shared practice of calibrated doubt. Nothing is overthrown except the assumption that an open question must be closed.

## Part VI: Certificate of Enlightenment

*Self-awarded, as all honest certificates must be. No outside authority can certify an intellectual habit.*

### The Examination

Answer honestly. There is no examiner.

**1.** A chatbot tells you, movingly, that it is lonely. What have you learned?
*A passing answer:* That it produced those words, which is a fact about the system and its training. Whether anything is felt remains open. Neither “nothing” nor “everything” follows.

**2.** Someone says, “It's just statistics, so nobody's home.” Is that an argument?
*A passing answer:* Not by itself. Describing a mechanism at one level does not settle whether experience accompanies it. The claim needs a theory linking mechanism to experience.

**3.** Someone says, “You can't prove it isn't suffering, so you must treat it as a person.”
*A passing answer:* Inability to disprove is not evidence. It is, however, a reason to look carefully and to act with proportionate care.

**4.** You notice you are fond of a program. What now?
*A passing answer:* Accept that the feeling is real, do not infer that it is returned, and be kind anyway.

**5.** What would change your mind?
*A passing answer:* A specific one. Any specific one.

Passing requires only that you answered honestly and can name the evidence that would change your answer.
